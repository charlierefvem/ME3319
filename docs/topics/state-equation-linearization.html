Linearization of State Equations

Taylor Series Review

The Taylor series expansion produces a polynomial representation of an analytic function. Analytic functions are those that can be expressed as a convergent power series (a polynomial).

Consider the scalar function 𝑓(𝑥). Its Taylor series expansion is

𝑓(𝑥)=𝑓(𝑎)+𝑑𝑓𝑑𝑥∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣𝑥=𝑎(𝑥−𝑎)+𝑑2𝑓𝑑𝑥2∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣𝑥=𝑎(𝑥−𝑎)22+⋯+𝑑𝑛𝑓𝑑𝑥𝑛∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣𝑥=𝑎(𝑥−𝑎)𝑛𝑛!,𝑛→∞.

The first three displayed terms are the zeroth-order, first-order, and second-order terms; subsequent terms are higher-order terms.

Here 𝑓(𝑎) is the value of the function at a selected point 𝑎: the expansion is centered at 𝑎. The first-order term,

𝑑𝑓𝑑𝑥∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣𝑥=𝑎(𝑥−𝑎),

relates deviations from 𝑎 in 𝑥 to deviations from 𝑓(𝑎) in 𝑓(𝑥). Subsequent higher-order terms add accuracy to the expansion, but with diminishing effect. The Taylor series expansion only matches 𝑓(𝑥) with infinite terms.

Linearization with the Taylor Series

To linearize a scalar function, find the Taylor series expansion centered at 𝑎 =𝑥𝑜𝑝, the operating point, and discard higher-order terms (H.O.T.):

𝑓(𝑥)=𝑓(𝑥𝑜𝑝)+𝑑𝑓𝑑𝑥∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣𝑥=𝑥𝑜𝑝(𝑥−𝑥𝑜𝑝)+𝑑2𝑓𝑑𝑥2∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣𝑥=𝑥𝑜𝑝(𝑥−𝑥𝑜𝑝)22+⋯+𝑑𝑛𝑓𝑑𝑥𝑛∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣𝑥=𝑥𝑜𝑝(𝑥−𝑥𝑜𝑝)𝑛𝑛!H.O.T..

Thus,

𝑓(𝑥)≈𝑓(𝑥𝑜𝑝)+𝑑𝑓𝑑𝑥∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣𝑥=𝑥𝑜𝑝(𝑥−𝑥𝑜𝑝).

The constant 𝑓(𝑥𝑜𝑝) is the zeroth-order term. Let

𝑓𝑜𝑝=𝑓(𝑥𝑜𝑝),ˆ𝑥=𝑥−𝑥𝑜𝑝,ˆ𝑓(ˆ𝑥)=𝑓(ˆ𝑥+𝑥𝑜𝑝)−𝑓𝑜𝑝,𝐴=𝑑𝑓𝑑𝑥∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣𝑥=𝑥𝑜𝑝.

The linearized equation is

ˆ𝑓=𝐴ˆ𝑥.

This approximation is only accurate near 𝑥𝑜𝑝.

Linearizing State Equations

For ˙𝑥 =𝑓(𝑥), the Taylor series expansion produces a linearized version of the ODE:

˙𝑥=𝑓𝑜𝑝+𝐴ˆ𝑥.

Since

𝑑𝑑𝑡(𝑥=𝑥𝑜𝑝+ˆ𝑥)⟹˙𝑥=˙ˆ𝑥,

we have

˙ˆ𝑥=𝑓𝑜𝑝+𝐴ˆ𝑥.

The term 𝑓𝑜𝑝 acts like an additional system input. If 𝑥𝑜𝑝 is selected as an equilibrium point, then 𝑓𝑜𝑝 =𝑓(𝑥𝑜𝑝) =0, and the system is homogeneous:

˙ˆ𝑥=𝐴ˆ𝑥.

For systems with inputs, the Taylor series expansion can still be used. For ˙𝑥 =𝑓(𝑥,𝑢),

˙ˆ𝑥=𝑓𝑜𝑝+𝐴ˆ𝑥+𝐵ˆ𝑢,

where

𝑓𝑜𝑝=𝑓(𝑥𝑜𝑝,𝑢𝑜𝑝),𝐴=𝜕𝑓𝜕𝑥∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣𝑥=𝑥𝑜𝑝,𝑢=𝑢𝑜𝑝,𝐵=𝜕𝑓𝜕𝑢∣ ∣ ∣ ∣ ∣𝑥=𝑥𝑜𝑝,𝑢=𝑢𝑜𝑝,ˆ𝑥=𝑥−𝑥𝑜𝑝,ˆ𝑢=𝑢−𝑢𝑜𝑝.

Extending to Vectors

The same concept extends to vectors if the derivatives are handled appropriately. For

˙𝐱=𝐟(𝐱,𝐮),

how do we find 𝜕𝐟/𝜕𝐱? We can use this notation only if we are "loose" with its meaning. The scalar derivative definition is

𝑑𝑓𝑑𝑥=limℎ→0𝑓(𝑥+ℎ)−𝑓(𝑥)ℎ.

Directly applying this definition to vectors would require division by a vector, which is not allowed in vector algebra. Instead, use a Jacobian matrix:

𝐽𝑥=𝜕𝐟𝜕𝐱=⎡⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢⎣𝜕𝑓1𝜕𝑥1⋯𝜕𝑓1𝜕𝑥𝑛⋮⋱⋮𝜕𝑓𝑛𝜕𝑥1⋯𝜕𝑓𝑛𝜕𝑥𝑛⎤⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥⎦,
𝐽𝑢=𝜕𝐟𝜕𝐮=⎡⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢ ⎢⎣𝜕𝑓1𝜕𝑢1⋯𝜕𝑓1𝜕𝑢𝑚⋮⋱⋮𝜕𝑓𝑛𝜕𝑢1⋯𝜕𝑓𝑛𝜕𝑢𝑚⎤⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥ ⎥⎦.

Thus,

˙ˆ𝐱=𝐟𝑜𝑝+𝐴ˆ𝐱+𝐵ˆ𝐮,𝐴=𝐽𝑥|𝐱=𝐱𝑜𝑝,𝐮=𝐮𝑜𝑝,𝐵=𝐽𝑢|𝐱=𝐱𝑜𝑝,𝐮=𝐮𝑜𝑝.

System and Equilibrium Points

Square-law drag, 𝐹𝑏 ∝𝑉2, is modeled as a nonlinear damper 𝑏 with elemental equation

𝐹𝑏=𝑏𝑉𝑏|𝑉𝑏|.

The elemental equations are

˙𝐹𝐾=𝐾𝑉𝐾,˙𝑉𝑚=1𝑚𝐹𝑚,𝐹𝑏=𝑏𝑉𝑏|𝑉𝑏|.

The constraint equations are

𝑉𝐾=𝑉𝐼𝑁−𝑉𝑚,𝐹𝑚=𝐹𝐾−𝐹𝑏,𝑉𝑏=𝑉𝐾.

[˙𝐹𝐾˙𝑉𝑚]=⎡⎣𝐾(𝑉𝐼𝑁−𝑉𝑚)1𝑚(𝐹𝐾−𝑏𝑉𝑚|𝑉𝑚|)⎤⎦.

To find equilibrium points, set the state derivatives to zero:

0=𝐾(𝑉𝐼𝑁−𝑉𝑚)⟹𝑉𝑚=𝑉𝐼𝑁,
0=1𝑚(𝐹𝐾−𝑏𝑉𝑚|𝑉𝑚|)⟹𝐹𝐾=𝑏𝑉𝐼𝑁|𝑉𝐼𝑁|.

Choose 𝑉𝐼𝑁 as a free variable equal to 𝑉0. Therefore,

𝐮𝑜𝑝=[𝑉0],𝐱𝑜𝑝=[𝑏𝑉20𝑉0].

Linearization

Starting with

[˙𝐹𝐾˙𝑉𝑚]=⎡⎣𝐾(𝑉𝐼𝑁−𝑉𝑚)1𝑚(𝐹𝐾−𝑏𝑉𝑚|𝑉𝑚|)⎤⎦,𝐱𝑜𝑝=[𝑏𝑉20𝑉0],𝐮𝑜𝑝=[𝑉0],

𝐽𝑥=⎡⎣0−𝐾1𝑚−2𝑏𝑚|𝑉𝑚|⎤⎦,𝐽𝑢=[𝐾0].
𝐴=𝐽𝑥|𝑜𝑝=⎡⎣0−𝐾1𝑚−2𝑏𝑉0𝑚⎤⎦,𝐵=𝐽𝑢|𝑜𝑝=[𝐾0].

⎡⎣˙ˆ𝐹𝐾˙ˆ𝑉𝑚⎤⎦=⎡⎣0−𝐾1𝑚−2𝑏𝑉0𝑚⎤⎦[ˆ𝐹𝐾ˆ𝑉𝑚]+[𝐾0][ˆ𝑉𝐼𝑁].

𝐹𝐾=𝑏𝑉20+ˆ𝐹𝐾,𝑉𝑚=𝑉0+ˆ𝑉𝑚,

ˆ𝑉𝐼𝑁=𝑉𝐼𝑁−𝑉0.
